What you just scrolled through

A stylised media campus — the long glass hull, the sky terrace on its colonnade, the theatre drums, the satellite dish compound out back. Nothing here was downloaded. There is no model file and no image file: every mesh is written down in code, and every texture is painted into a <canvas> at runtime by a seeded generator, so the same 16 window tints and the same blades of grass come back on every load.

02

How it works

Four moving parts: a scroll timeline, a camera spline, three lighting rigs, and an exporter.

The timeline

Scroll is the build order

Parts are sorted by phase, then by height, and each phase owns a slice of the scroll. Every part flies in from its own random point in the sky — position, rotation and scale all easing home, the scale with a slight overshoot. Scroll back up and the campus takes itself apart in the same order.

The camera

One spline, seven waypoints

A Catmull-Rom curve carries the camera from the south approach, around the back, and home again, while the look-at target climbs as the building grows. Drag at any time to take over — the drone orbit button hands it to a wandering radius-and-height loop instead.

The light

Morning, noon, dusk

Each preset paints its own sky gradient, then bakes that sky plus a sun sphere into a PMREM environment map — which is what the glass and bronze actually reflect. A sun sprite tracks the key light; at dusk it is swapped for a moon and 320 stars, the clouds turn slate, and the window emissives, the media screen and the street lamps come up.

The sky

Weather, cheaply

Fourteen clouds, each a handful of squashed spheres, drift across the sky on their own clock and wrap around — and five flattened spheres sit past the fog line as hills, so the horizon is a landscape rather than a hard edge.

The output

It leaves as a real model

Every mesh and material is named, so the campus exports straight from the browser as GLB or as OBJ + MTL — in metres, y-up, ready to open in Blender.

A likeness, not a survey. The proportions are drawn from the kind of media campus I walk into — a long curved hull over a green-glass podium, drums for the studios — but the geometry is hand-written, not measured. Rendering pauses whenever the stage scrolls out of view, and the whole thing falls back to a single still frame if you have reduced motion turned on.
